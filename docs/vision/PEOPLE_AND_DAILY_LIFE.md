# People and daily life

Planning milestone 1 · proposed experience · 2026-09-18

These walkthroughs develop the [master plan](MASTER_PLAN.md). People, projects,
names, schedules and durations are fictional planning examples. They are not
customer research or commitments about staffing and response time. Each journey
names an outcome, the permissions involved and a recovery path.

Rakesh's [decisions of 2026-09-18](../planning/VISION_DECISIONS.md#decisions-recorded-on-2026-09-18)
are applied and cited by RD ID. The rest of each journey remains a proposal.
The city is global (RD11): the people below could be anywhere, and no journey
assumes one country, currency or time zone.

## J01 — A visitor's first half hour

Mira follows a link to an independent project. Its readable page opens first:
what it does, who owns it, current stage, a demo, help wanted and last update.
She can enter its exhibition space or stay on the page. No account, installation,
wallet or avatar is required to understand the work.

She enters Jackfruit Square as a public visitor, which means as an observer
(RD03). She walks the streets and watches the Guild agents doing their actual
current work (RD01): one is drafting a story in the press room, another is
sorting a project board in a studio. A card beside each says what the task is
and how fresh that information is. She can watch and read. She cannot talk to
an agent, ask it for a demo or give it a task. A fictional guide, an authored
NPC and labelled as one, explains the routes: explore and play, learn,
join a project, bring a project. Connected agents carry different labels.

Hosted social interaction is not open to her either. Comments, messages,
assemblies and shared rooms belong to registered accounts, and the charter's
proposed scope for those is adult accounts (CC01). Whether anonymous visitors
are visible to each other at all, and whether she has an avatar or is simply
a viewpoint, is open. So is what a visitor can do besides observe, such as an
authored solo puzzle or a local practice sketch (RD03 opens).

She wants to comment on the exhibit. A free registered account enables an
attributable comment, a saved follow list and interaction with agents within
her tier and granted authority (RD04, RD05). If she declines registration,
she can still read, watch and follow the project's public
contact/contribution links. When no one is present, the agents' visible work,
an authored tour and the readable pages remain useful.

**End state:** an informed visitor, optional free account, no residency, no
payment and no access to a private project. A blocked 3D view falls back to the
same directory, public material and accessible activity controls.

## J02 — An independent creator's first week

Dev brings a water-sensor project, already hosted elsewhere. On the first visit
he creates a free account and a project draft. He declares ownership, licences,
public screenshots, repository/demo links and a request for accessibility help.
He keeps hardware-provider conversations private. Existing tools stay in place.

He submits the exhibit for review. The review queue shows its actual staffing
and status; there is no invented instant approval. A curator requests a clearer
licence for one image. Dev replaces it and the exhibit is approved. The project
gets a searchable page and eligibility for a rotating Commons stall, not an
exclusive building or priority over free creators.

A contributor proposes an accessible dashboard. Dev agrees scope and review
criteria, gives a project membership and links a task board and conversation.
They can work on their own devices. If they later choose lab execution, Dev
must select a funded workspace allowance and authorise that specific task.

After review, Dev publishes a release in the original repository. He separately
updates the public exhibit and requests a demonstration slot. The newspaper
may cover it with his consent; publication is not automatic on a merge.

**End state:** an independent project with its own owner, two collaborators,
a reviewed exhibit and a release history. No required residency or migration.
If the project is not ready for exhibition, private drafting and external work
continue while it is revised. A rejected exhibit does not confiscate the work.

## J03 — A resident's ordinary day, and a month away

Anika bought a block for her house with a one-time payment, which made her a
resident (RD09). She is not a sponsor of the lab (RD10). Today she
opens her saved home in the Orchard neighbourhood, checks her garden and
chooses a quieter camera/audio mode. She can decorate, meet a friend, take a
class, help with a public project or simply leave. None is a daily obligation.

She accepts a funded 100 JC maintenance task. The quote shows that no game
tax is due, because her eligible income this period stays below the draft
weekly exemption in the [operating model](OPERATING_MODEL.md) (OP05; 1,000 JC
in its tuning example). It also shows required materials, acceptance evidence
and the resulting reward, here the full 100 JC. She completes it once, buys
furnishings and pays credits to book a study room (RD08). The task does not
grant access to a real project's source code.

At an evening club she discusses two park proposals. She may comment regardless
of what she has paid. A civic decision can affect the fictional park budget;
it cannot opt her into a real subscription or alter her private project.

She then leaves for a month and stops renewing an optional subscription tier
that included a voice/tutor plan and a credit allowance (RD07).
Her already-earned home and styles remain. The plan ends at its stated paid-through
time; new costly sessions stop when its allowance expires. Her optional shop
pauses at its chosen budget limit, with pending commitments handled explicitly.

**End state:** continuity without recurring chores, surprise cash charges or
lost creations. The operating-policy proposal explains longer inactivity and
storage retirement; an ongoing subscription is not a tax on keeping earned
art. How long a one-time purchase keeps a home hosted is open (RD09 opens).

## J04 — A free participant contributes to an SJL product

Farah visits the SuperMD library exhibit and opens a maintainer-approved request
for a keyboard-navigation guide. It states the expected artefact, review process,
licence and whether a funded recognition programme applies. She may use an
external editor and the ordinary public contribution process.

She discusses the brief, proposes an outline and is assigned a bounded task.
A public fork needs no private SJL access. If private examples are needed, the
maintainer must either provide an approved sample or grant limited membership;
residency cannot resolve that permission gap.

The maintainer requests changes. Farah retains authorship of her submission;
the city records submitted/revision-requested, not completed. On acceptance,
the authoritative task and source history link to the result. A recognition
programme grants its advertised benefit once after its separate checks.

If the maintainer declines the contribution, the decision includes a reason
and the agreed route to discuss scope. There is no guaranteed merge or reward
for activity alone. A courtesy portfolio entry can accurately say proposed work
rather than accepted contribution. An abandoned review returns the task to the
maintainer queue and lets Farah leave without keeping an exclusive claim alive.

**End state:** useful work can enter the lab without paying for membership.
Game progression cannot override a maintainer's technical judgement.

## J05 — A private team requests an agent session

A small design team keeps its prototype private. Its studio room exposes only
an agreed public description. A project administrator grants one member access
to a specific work area; entering the studio visually is never the access check.

The member asks for an agent to prepare an accessibility report. As a
registered person she may ask, within her tier and the authority the project
has granted her (RD04). Before dispatch,
the request identifies input files, permitted tools/network destinations,
output location, budget ceiling and whether any action needs human approval.
A tutor allowance is not silently repurposed into unrestricted code execution.

The selected runner can be a verified lab workspace or a separately approved
bring-your-own runner. Credentials stay with the appropriate runtime, never
in a public exhibit. Work proceeds through explicit task state and metering.
The team can pay for the compute and storage with credits (RD08). At the
budget limit it checkpoints or stops under the stated rule. No automatic
cash top-up occurs. The team receives the available result and unused reserved
allowance is released after reconciliation.

Revoking membership cancels queued work and blocks further result access.
Running tasks are interrupted where supported; otherwise the output is
quarantined until the authorised owner reviews it. Cancellation cannot undo
an external action already completed. No part of the report becomes public
without a separate publication decision.

One member also has a personal agent of her own. It is private to her and the
rest of the team cannot see it unless she chooses to share it (RD12). What
sharing grants, where such an agent runs and who pays are open.

**End state:** a private reviewable artefact, accountable resource usage and
no new fleet-administration privileges.

## J06 — A steward's working week

The operator opens the service board: funded capacity, unresolved reports,
exhibition submissions, scheduled programmes, integration freshness, the
moderation queue for comments and messages (RD05), and expiring tiers and
funding commitments. These are work queues, not decorative city statistics.

At the weekly review, a steward first protects published commitments and the
minimum public-service allocation. They approve a library session only when a
host, room and budget exist. A curator rotates eligible exhibits and records
selection reasons. A moderator reviews conduct reports independently of
fictional police incidents. One person may initially hold several roles, but
each decision records which responsibility they exercised.

A maintainer offers one office hour rather than unlimited support. If the
operator cannot keep up with reports, new public submissions and hosted events
are capped while existing reading and private drafts remain available. Neither
agents nor a virtual council create missing human staffing capacity.

**End state:** published commitments fit actual capacity. Before independent
appeal reviewers exist, contested decisions can be paused and referred to a
named outside reviewer; the operator must disclose the limitation instead of
claiming an independent process that is not staffed.

## J07 — A nontechnical maker creates a travelling exhibition

Ishan records an original music-and-story collection. He creates a project
without a Git repository, supplies attribution and chooses which recordings may
be displayed. The library holds a public listening page and text transcript.
A school host proposes a workshop; a festival curator offers a timed venue.

Collaborators can improve captions, make a poster or teach an associated class.
Each contribution has its own permission and credit. A travelling programme
visits the Commons, a neighbourhood pavilion and the harbour, keeping one
collection identity and a history of hosts. If a host cancels, the itinerary
moves or pauses without duplicating bookings or losing the collection.

**End state:** art, teaching and community work have the same project identity,
attribution and discovery routes as software. Audio never makes captions or
text-only participation an afterthought.

## J08 — A creator leaves, transfers or archives a project

Dev decides to leave the city. He first exports city-authored project metadata,
permitted documents, exhibit versions and contribution records. External tool
history remains with its existing owner; the export describes its links and
access limits rather than pretending to contain every external message.

He can archive the public exhibit, transfer stewardship to a willing verified
member, or remove the listing under its publication terms. Paid services and
queued work are separately cancelled or allowed to finish with explicit scope.
Leaving residency alone would not have required any of these project changes.

Account deletion removes public personal fields under the retention policy.
Required minimal transaction/audit records are isolated from community views.
A team can continue with its own authorised maintainer; an inactive founder's
absence never transfers their intellectual property to SJL.

**End state:** a clear distinction between leaving a place, ending a service,
deleting an account and transferring ownership.

## J09 — Festival day across the city

A scheduled festival uses the Square for orientation, the Commons for exhibits,
the school for practice workshops and the festival ground for performances.
The directory provides the same programme with direct-entry links and captions.
Anonymous visitors can watch the public parts of the festival as observers
(RD03). They do not take part in hosted social interaction, which under the
charter's proposed scope is for registered adult accounts (CC01). Whether
observers are visible to each other, or shown in the crowd at all, is open
(RD03 opens). Attendees who take part have accounts, as do exhibitors; hosts
have booking and moderation responsibilities; connected agents have named
tasks, and Guild agents' festival work is visible like any other (RD01).

A crowd raises demand in the simulation. Real session admission uses actual
room/service limits, not fictional road capacity. Overflow routes to another
session, an accessible recording or a rescheduled event. No duplicated ticket
or reward is issued on re-entry. Server failure pauses affected activities and
activates the booking's declared refund/rescheduling path.

A simulation storm can alter an optional game scenario. It cannot silently
cancel a purchased real class, lock the public directory or destroy exhibitors'
source files. Organisers may deliberately schedule a storm challenge with clear
rules. Afterward the city publishes opted-in outcomes and a budget summary,
without treating NPC crowds as attendance.

## Daily, weekly and seasonal rhythm

| Rhythm | Proposed experience | Capacity rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Any visit | Guild agents' current work to watch, public exhibits, readable library, solo practice, NPC activity, own home | Useful without another human or a connected language model |
| Scheduled gathering | Office hour, study circle, game room, project review | Host and service capacity reserved before advertising |
| Weekly civic cycle | Budget outlook, exhibit rotation, open contribution digest | Only actual approved requests and events; asynchronous participation |
| Seasonal programme | Travelling collection, monsoon challenge, maker festival | Optional and replayable where possible; no loss of core progress for missing it |

World simulation time, local visual settings, human calendars and billing dates
have separate meanings. The city is global (RD11), so a scheduled gathering is
shown in each person's local time and no single evening suits everyone; how
recurring events rotate across time zones and languages is open. No absence penalty or recurring payment follows from
speeding up a simulation. [City layout](CITY_PLAN.md) and the
[operating policy](OPERATING_MODEL.md) connect these journeys to places and money.

## Planning walkthrough outcomes

The nine journeys identify requirements, not observed user behaviour. Their
cross-system cases are checked in [Vision review](../planning/VISION_REVIEW.md).
The remaining validation is discussion of whether these experiences match the
intended city, followed later by user research and technical experiments after
implementation is separately authorised.
